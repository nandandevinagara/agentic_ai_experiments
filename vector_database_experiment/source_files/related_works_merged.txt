2.2 VLM Efficiency and Token Reduction (merged draft)

The quadratic complexity of attention mechanisms in VLMs with respect to the number of visual tokens poses a significant computational bottleneck, motivating a range of token- and resolution-reduction strategies. FastV [CZL+24] removes visual tokens after the second LLM layer, while SparseVLM [ZFM+25] employs a text-guided, training-free sparsification strategy that ranks and prunes visual tokens based on their relevance to the input query and recycles pruned tokens to minimize information loss. Token merging [BFD+23] takes a complementary approach, combining redundant tokens into fewer representative ones rather than discarding visual information outright. At the input level, resolution-reduction methods constrain the pixel budget available to the model before it ever reaches the encoder: "Seeing More with Less" [GUH25] shows that a fixed, center-based foveation strategy retaining only 3% of original pixels at high resolution (while heavily downsampling or discarding the remaining 97%) is sufficient to reach 80% of full-resolution model capacity on visual question answering and object detection tasks, though computational efficiency gains are not explicitly claimed. TransNeXt (CITE) similarly introduces a foveal-inspired attention design, combining fine-grained pixel-level attention with coarse, low-resolution peripheral attention (Aggregated Attention) and a convolutional channel mixer (Convolutional GLU), mimicking biological visual scanning. However, both [GUH25] and TransNeXt apply their foveal structure independent of image content or question semantics, and SparseVLM, FastV, and token merging all operate on visual tokens that are initially encoded at uniform resolution. While these methods achieve meaningful efficiency gains, none of them account for the spatial relevance of image regions with respect to the input question, limiting adaptability to diverse question types and spatial object distributions.

Saliency-based methods address part of this gap by identifying visually prominent regions prior to model inference, independent of token-pruning considerations. Zhao and Koch [ZK12] frame saliency prediction as a classification problem trained on human fixation data, demonstrating that learned feature combinations can reliably predict where humans look in a scene and providing an early foundation for Region of Interest (ROI) selection. Extending this to structural scene understanding, Zhu et al. [ZHL18] propose a salient object detection framework for panoramic images, showing that integrating eye-fixation cues with spatial density patterns yields more reliable region detection. Yet these saliency-based approaches remain bottom-up and question-agnostic: SparseVLM's text-guided significance estimation shows that different questions require attention to different visual regions, supporting the hypothesis that query-conditional foveation, rather than purely stimulus-driven saliency, is what is needed for efficient and accurate VLM inference. This gap — task-agnostic token/resolution reduction on one side, and question-agnostic saliency on the other — motivates a question-conditional foveation module that learns from gaze-question pairs during training.
